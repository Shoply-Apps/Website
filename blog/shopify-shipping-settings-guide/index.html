Shopify shipping settings explained: zones, rates and delivery times

By the Shoply Apps team · 20 October 2026 · 10 min read

Key takeaways

  • Shopify shipping settings are built from shipping profiles (groups of products), locations (where you ship from), zones (where you ship to) and rates (what customers pay).
  • Every zone needs at least one rate; if a customer's address is in no zone, or a zone has no rate, they cannot check out.
  • Use flat rates when products weigh about the same and weight-based or carrier-calculated rates when weights vary; carrier rates depend on your plan and region.
  • Enter a real weight for every variant, including packaging, and set rates that cover what shipping actually costs you.
  • Show processing plus transit time on product pages and at checkout, and say who pays duties on international orders.

Shopify shipping settings decide which customers can buy from you, what they pay for delivery and what they are told about when it will arrive. If they are set up badly, you either lose money on every parcel or lose the sale at checkout. This guide walks through how it all works in plain language. Menu names change over time, so if a label looks different in your admin, look for the closest match under your shipping and delivery settings.

How does shipping work in Shopify?

Shopify works out a shipping price at checkout by matching the products in the cart, the customer's address and the rates you have created. It all lives in the shipping and delivery section of your settings.

The building blocks, from biggest to smallest:

  • Shipping profile: a group of products that share the same shipping rules.
  • Location: a place you ship from, such as your home, a warehouse or a fulfillment partner.
  • Shipping zone: a group of countries or regions you ship to.
  • Shipping rate: the price and service a customer in that zone can choose, such as "Standard, $5".

When a customer enters their address, Shopify finds the matching zone and shows the rates that apply to the cart. If no zone covers the address, the customer cannot complete the order.

What are shipping profiles, and when do you need a custom one?

Every store starts with a general shipping profile, and new products go into it automatically. You only need a custom profile when some products ship in a genuinely different way from the rest.

Typical reasons: heavy or bulky items that cost far more to send, fragile items that need special packaging or a specific courier, products shipped from a different place, and products you only sell locally, such as fresh food.

Keep it simple: each extra profile is another set of zones and rates to maintain. Many small stores only need the general profile.

Which locations do you ship from?

A location is any address that holds stock or fulfills orders. Shopify uses your locations to decide where an order ships from, and carrier-calculated rates use the origin address to work out distance.

Check that each address is correct, including the postal code, and that locations which should fulfill online orders are set to do so.

How do you set up shipping zones?

A shipping zone is a list of countries or regions that share the same rates. You create zones inside a shipping profile, then add at least one rate to each zone.

A sensible starting set for a small store:

  1. Domestic: your own country.
  2. Nearby countries: neighbours or a trading region where delivery is cheaper and faster.
  3. Rest of world: only if you are ready to handle longer delivery times and import charges.

Only add countries you can really deliver to at a price that works, and give every zone at least one rate.

What types of Shopify shipping rates can you use?

Shopify shipping rates come in two broad families: rates you set yourself, and rates calculated by a carrier at checkout. You can mix them in the same zone, for example a free standard option and a paid express option.

Flat rateOne fixed price per order, such as $6Products of similar size and weight

Should you offer local delivery or local pickup?

If customers can reach your shop, studio or stock room, local options are worth turning on. You set them up per location.

  • Local delivery: you deliver orders yourself within an area you define, such as by postal code or distance, with your own price and minimum order.
  • Local pickup: customers collect from your location, usually free, and you tell them when it will be ready.

Why do product weights matter for shipping?

Weight-based and carrier-calculated rates both depend on weight, and Shopify uses the weight entered on each product variant. If a weight is missing or zero, the cart looks lighter than it is and the customer is charged too little.

  • Weigh each variant. A large size or a set of three weighs more than the single item.
  • Include packaging. Boxes, padding and mailers add weight. Shopify lets you save package sizes and a default package weight in your shipping settings, so check that it matches what you really use.
  • Check imports. Products added by CSV or from a supplier often arrive with no weight at all.

What is dimensional weight?

Dimensional (or volumetric) weight is a weight carriers calculate from a parcel's size. Many carriers charge for whichever is higher, real or dimensional weight, so a large, light box can cost more than you expect. Use the smallest box that protects the product and check your carrier's rules.

Flat rate or weight-based? A worked example

For a small store, flat rate is simplest when products weigh about the same; weight-based rates are safer when weights vary widely. Here is an example with made-up numbers for a candle store that ships domestically.

Example postage costs the store pays its carrier (including packaging):

Parcel weightExample postage costExample orders per month
Up to 0.5 kg$560
0.5 to 2 kg$830
2 to 5 kg$1410

Monthly postage cost = (60 × $5) + (30 × $8) + (10 × $14) = $300 + $240 + $140 = $680

Option A: flat rate of $6. The store collects 100 × $6 = $600 and pays $680, so it loses $80 a month on shipping. Every heavy order loses $8, and light-order customers pay $1 more than the real cost.

Option B: weight-based rates of $5, $8 and $14 matching the table. The store collects exactly $680 and breaks even on shipping. Light-order customers see a lower price, which can help them buy.

Here weight-based rates fit best because parcels range from under 0.5 kg to 5 kg. If nearly all orders were under 0.5 kg, a flat rate would be simpler and just as good. Packaging matters too: a 0.45 kg candle in a 0.1 kg box ships as 0.55 kg and lands in the next band.

How should you show delivery times?

Show realistic delivery times on your product pages and at checkout, and add your processing time to the transit time. A vague or missing answer makes shoppers hesitate.

  • Name rates clearly, for example "Standard (5 to 8 business days)" rather than "Shipping".
  • Count processing time. If you need two days to pack an order, a "3-day" rate really takes five.
  • Say it on the product page and in a shipping policy page, so customers do not have to start checkout to find out.
  • Be honest about busy periods and international orders.

Unclear delivery information is one of the conversion problems covered in our guide on how to increase your Shopify conversion rate.

What should you know about international shipping?

International orders take longer, cost more and may be charged duties and import taxes when they arrive. Who pays those charges depends on how you set up your store and checkout, so decide that up front and tell customers clearly.

  • State on your shipping policy page whether the price includes duties and taxes or whether the customer may be charged on delivery.
  • Expect customs paperwork; check what your carrier needs.
  • Check that your products can legally be sent to each country you add.
  • Register your business and handle sales tax or VAT as your country and your customers' countries require; ask a local accountant if you are unsure.

How should dropshippers set up shipping?

Dropshippers should set rates that cover what their supplier really charges for delivery, not what feels attractive. If you are new to the model, our step-by-step guide to starting dropshipping on Shopify covers the basics.

  • Multiple suppliers mean multiple parcels. An order with items from two suppliers usually means two shipping charges for you, even if the customer paid once.
  • Supplier shipping costs change. Recheck them regularly and update your rates.
  • Show real delivery times: the supplier's processing time plus transit.
  • Free shipping must fit inside your product margin.

What are the most common Shopify shipping mistakes?

Most shipping problems come from a handful of gaps that are easy to miss when you set up quickly.

  • Missing weights. Variants with no weight make weight-based and carrier rates too cheap.
  • Zones with no rates. Customers in that zone reach checkout and cannot pay, which is a lost sale you may never notice.
  • Rates below your real cost. Every order quietly eats into margin. Combined with discounts, a product can end up selling at a loss; our guide on finding products you're selling below cost shows how to check.
  • Unclear delivery times. Shoppers who cannot see when an order will arrive often leave instead of asking.

Shipping costs only hurt less when the product price leaves room for them. FAQ

Why can't some customers check out on my Shopify store?

Usually because their address is not in any shipping zone, or the zone has no rate for the products in their cart. Add the country or region to a zone and give every zone in every shipping profile at least one rate.

Does Shopify use product weight to calculate shipping?

Yes. Weight-based rates and carrier-calculated rates use the weight entered on each product variant, plus any packaging weight you set. Missing or zero weights make shipping look cheaper than it really is.

Should I use flat rate or weight-based shipping?

Use a flat rate if your products are similar in size and weight. Use weight-based rates if weights vary a lot, so heavy orders do not cost you more to ship than the customer pays.

Who pays duties and import taxes on international orders?

It depends on how you set up your store and checkout. Either you include them in the price or the customer may be charged on delivery. Decide up front and state it clearly in your shipping policy.
